## Method

All 8 years (2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021) and all 1848 API-call features were used. The features are non-negative integer counts, so drift was measured with measures designed for counts, not binary data. For **every one of the 28 year pairs** (each year against the first year, year against the next year, and every other pair) and separately for **all apps, benign apps and malware apps**, each feature was compared with:

- **Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic** (the assignment's suggestion): the largest gap between the two empirical distributions, from 0 (same) to 1 (completely different). The p-value is the asymptotic one. Integer counts contain many ties (most features are 0 for most apps), so this p-value is only approximate and is not treated as exact.
- **Change in prevalence**: the share of apps with a nonzero count of the API call in the later year minus the earlier year. It is easy to interpret.
- **Jensen-Shannon (JS) distance** between the binned count distributions (log2 bins: 0, 1, 2, 3-4, 5-8, 9-16, ...; base 2, so 0 to 1). It reacts to changes in how often a call is made, not only to whether it is used.
- **Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction** across the 1848 features, applied separately for each year pair and each class (that is the correction family). A feature is called "significant" when its BH-adjusted p-value is below 0.05.
- **Effect sizes next to significance.** Because the samples are large (up to 18,000 rows per year), many small differences are statistically significant, so the tables also give the number of features with KS >= 0.2. That cut-off is a descriptive convention chosen in advance, not a tuned value.

**Data available per year** (measured from the loaded files; `results/step2_year_stats.csv`). The three counts are different things: rows, distinct feature vectors, and distinct app identifiers. The classifier sample sizes further below are none of these, they are how many vectors that comparison drew.

Two more checks:

- **Identical feature vectors.** The main numbers keep every row, because Step 1 found many repeated rows and the student has not yet decided whether to drop them. As a sensitivity check the year-to-year comparison was repeated with identical feature vectors collapsed inside each year and class (`results/step2_dedup_sensitivity.csv`). This is feature-vector deduplication, not application deduplication: two different apps can share a feature vector, so an identical vector is not evidence of a repeated app. Duplicate `apkname` identifiers are counted separately in Step 1, and the table above reports rows, distinct vectors and distinct identifiers side by side.
- **Temporal distinguishability (year classifier).** For a pair of years, a logistic regression (on log(1 + count) features, standardised inside each training split) was trained to tell the two years apart, and the held-out ROC AUC is reported (0.5 = the years look the same, 1.0 = perfectly separable). Identical feature vectors are collapsed inside each year and class first, both years are sampled to the same size (at most 2,000 vectors each in this run), and the train/test split is **group-aware**: one group per distinct feature vector across the two compared years, so a vector that occurs in both years stays on one side of every split. Each comparison is repeated over 5 such splits and the table reports the mean and standard deviation of the AUC. A comparison whose years hold fewer than 30 distinct vectors is not run and is shown as "not run" (0 of 54 comparisons). The split is random inside the two-year sample on purpose: the question is whether the years differ, not whether a model generalises to later years, so the AUC is a descriptive measure of how separable two years are and is not a temporal-generalisation result.

**What this analysis is not.** It is descriptive only. It was not used to choose the train/test split (2014-2017 for training, 2018-2021 for testing, fixed before this step), to select features, or to tune models. It shows that the **feature distributions** change over time (feature drift) and that years can be told apart. It does not by itself show concept drift (a change in the relationship between features and labels); that is discussed in Step 6 together with the change in predictive performance.

Limits to keep in mind: (1) sample size per year (the years are not the same size: 18,000 rows in 2014-2018 and 3,000 rows in 2019-2021) - the KS statistic is noisier (slightly larger by chance) for smaller samples, so mean KS values are best compared between pairs of similar sample size; (2) tied integer counts make KS p-values approximate; (3) repeated feature vectors inflate the effective sample size, and the table above shows how many distinct vectors each year actually holds.

- **Drift accumulates with the time gap.** The mean KS is 0.014 for 2014 vs 2015 and 0.052 for 2014 vs 2021; over all 28 year pairs the mean KS and the gap in years correlate at 0.78. The number of features with KS >= 0.2 grows from 7 to 199. Both classes drift: between 2014 and 2021, 223 features reach KS >= 0.2 for benign apps and 265 for malware.
- **The most different pair of years.** Taking the largest mean KS over all 28 pairs rather than the largest time gap, it is 2014 vs 2021 at mean KS 0.052, which is also the pair farthest apart in time.
- **Consecutive steps are mostly small and similar.** The mean KS between neighbouring years ranges from 0.014 (2014 to 2015) to 0.030 (2020 to 2021). The largest step is 2020 to 2021, and it is concentrated in malware (266 features with KS >= 0.2 for malware against 4 for the other class).
- **Duplicate sensitivity of that step.** In 2020 and 2021 the malware rows hold 102 and 129 distinct feature vectors, out of 1,500 and 1,500 rows. With identical vectors collapsed, the malware step 2020 to 2021 has 8 features with KS >= 0.2 and mean KS 0.025, against 266 and 0.054 with all rows. So a large share of this step reflects the weight of repeated feature vectors rather than many distinct vectors changing. Because identical vectors need not belong to the same app, that is a statement about the feature representation, not proof that the same apps recur. The collapsed samples are also small, so neither version is conclusive. This should feed the student's decision on duplicates.
- **The train/test boundary.** The step across the fixed boundary (2017 to 2018) has mean KS 0.014, which ranks 6 of 7 among the consecutive steps (0.014 to 0.030), so it is not an unusual single-year step. The larger drift between training and test comes from the test years being several years away from the training years (for example 2017 vs 2021: mean KS 0.048).

- **Pooled counts can hide class-specific change.** The clearest consecutive pair is 2020 to 2021: 266 features reach KS >= 0.2 in benign or malware apps, 187 of them with the benign and malware prevalence moving in strictly opposite directions (a change of exactly zero in either class is not counted), but only 82 reach it in the pooled data (all apps). ## What this means for the models

- **Expect a model trained on the first years to meet different data later on.** Feature drift is gradual and cumulative (mean KS 0.014 after one year, 0.052 after 7 years), and years several steps apart can be separated almost perfectly from the features alone. The test years (2018-2021) are further from the training years (2014-2017) the later they are, so Step 6 should show whether accuracy, and recall in particular, falls year by year. This step only shows that the inputs change; it does not show that accuracy falls.
- **Drift is not limited to one class.** Both benign and malware apps change, so it is not only a shift in the mix of apps. A shift in class balance is also ruled out as the cause here, since every year is 50% malware (per-year counts in the table above).
- **New API calls appear in the later years.** 55 features that were almost never used in 2014 are common in 2021. A model fitted on the training years only sees these calls at very low counts, and a scaler fitted on those years will give them unusual values on test data. This is one reason to expect a static model to age, and it is a reason to compare static training with retraining or incremental updates in Step 7 (the Step 7 scaler stays fixed on the training years as planned).
- **No change to the plan follows from this step.** The split stays 2014-2017 / 2018-2021, and no feature selection or tuning used these results. Whether the feature-label relationship itself changed (concept drift) is left to Step 6.
- **Open decision for the student: duplicates.** Identical feature vectors are numerous (the per-year table above), and they affect the drift numbers (see the 2020 to 2021 malware step). Nothing was dropped, and the two kinds of duplicate (identical vectors and repeated `apkname` values) are still reported separately.
